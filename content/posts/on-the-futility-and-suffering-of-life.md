## On the Longing for Unconsciousness
Upon awakening from the night of unconsciousness, the will finds itself as an individual in an endless and boundless world, among countless other individuals, all of whom struggle, suffer, and err; and as if through a troubled dream, it rushes back to its former unconsciousness.

## On Endless Desire
Its desires are boundless, its claims endless, and every desire that is satisfied gives birth to a new one. There is no satisfaction in this world that can sate its cravings, determine the ultimate goal of its demands, or fill the abyss of death within its heart.

## On What Satisfaction Is in Life
Let us consider what humans typically receive in the form of any kind of satisfaction; often this is nothing more than the maintenance of this existence, carried out daily with unceasing effort and in a slow, continuous struggle against misery and deprivation, and against the possibility of death. 

## On Life as a Deception
Everything in life suggests that worldly happiness is destined to be thwarted, or is regarded as an illusion. The basis for this lies in the very nature of all things. Therefore, most people’s lives prove to be troubled and short. Those who are relatively happy often only appear to be happy, or, like those who live long lives, they are rare exceptions; perhaps they should be left alone, like decoy birds. Life presents itself as a constant deception, in both small and great matters. If it has made a promise, it will not keep it, except to show how little it truly desires; therefore, we are now deceived by hope, now by what is hoped for.

## On the past, the present, and the future
The allure of distance shows us a paradise that vanishes like an optical illusion, when we allow ourselves to be deceived by it. Therefore, happiness always lies in the future or in the past, and the present can be likened to a small dark cloud driven by the wind across a sunny plain; in front of and behind the cloud everything is bright, only the cloud itself always casts a shadow. Consequently, the present is always inadequate, yet the future is uncertain, and the past cannot be restored. Misfortunes, both small and great, occur every hour, every day, every week, and every year; with deceptive hopes and accidents that render all calculations futile.

## On a life that deserves lamentation rather than gratitude
Life clearly bears the stamp of something that should make us feel repulsed, so it is hard to imagine how anyone could fail to realize this, and be convinced that life is here to be grateful for, enjoyed, and that humans exist to be happy. On the contrary, constant deception and disappointment, as well as the general nature of life, present themselves as something intended and calculated to instill the belief that nothing is commensurate with our efforts, struggles, and strivings; that all good things are futile, and fleeting, that the world is falling apart on all sides, and that life is merely a business unable to cover its own costs—so that our will may turn away from it.

## On Old Age and Death
Old age and death, which every living being inevitably experiences, are a punishment for the will to live that originates from nature itself. It is said that this will is a struggle that is bound to defeat itself. “What you will,” it says, “will end like this: will something better come to pass?” Therefore, the lesson imparted to every person through their life consists of the reality that the objects of their desire are constantly deceiving, faltering, and falling; as a result, they bring more suffering than joy, until eventually even the very foundation upon which they stand crumbles, for life itself is shattered. Thus he gains the final certainty that all his efforts and desires are a delusion, a path of error: Then old age and experience go hand in hand, leading him toward death, and making him realize, after such a painful and long search, that throughout his life he has been on the wrong side.

## On the Positive and the Negative
We feel pain, but not without pain; concern, but not freedom from concern; fear, but not safety and security. We feel these desires just as we feel hunger and thirst; but once the hunger is satisfied, the food is like a mouthful that has been eaten, and we feel nothing more once it is swallowed. We sadly feel the loss of pleasure and enjoyment as soon as they cease to be present; but when pain ceases—even after having been present for a long time—its absence is not felt directly, but at most felt intentionally through reflection. For only pain and deprivation can be felt positively; and therefore they make themselves felt; well-being, on the other hand, is merely negative.

## On the Presence of Time
The hours pass—the more pleasantly they are spent, the faster they go; the more painfully they are spent, the slower they go—because pain, not pleasure, is the positive thing whose presence makes itself felt. We become aware of time when we are bored, not when we are entertained. Our existence is most joyful when we are least aware of it; from this, it follows that it would be better not to have it at all.

## On Good and Evil
There is no point in debating whether there is more good or evil in this world; for the very existence of evil is what defines the problem. Since evil can never be eradicated, its consequences can never be balanced by the good that exists alongside it or follows it. For thousands of people living in happiness and joy can never erase the suffering and misfortune of a single person’s death; and my current well-being cannot erase my past suffering. Therefore, even if evil in this world were a hundred times smaller than it actually is, its mere existence would still be enough to establish a truth that can be expressed in various ways—though always only indirectly—namely, that we should not feel joy, but rather lament the existence of the world; that its nonexistence would be better than its existence; that it is something that, fundamentally, should not have come to be, and so on.

## On Byron’s quote about life
Our life is a flaw—out of harmony with all things, this harsh decree, this indelible stain of sin, this boundless Upas, this mighty tree, whose roots are in the earth, whose leaves and branches are the sky, which rains down plagues upon humanity like dew — Disease, death, slavery—all the miseries we see—And worse still, the miseries we do not see—which afflict the incurable soul with ever-renewed heartache. (Childe Harold, iv, 126)
